The basics

Facts about farming in Brazil

An agricultural powerhouse

Brazil is one of the world's great agricultural nations. Within 40 years, it transformed itself from a net food importer into one of the largest exporters of agricultural commodities on earth — driven above all by the liberalization of the sector.

Brazilian farmers operate on the free market. There are no state subsidies and no guaranteed prices, and in general there are fewer rules to observe than in Europe. This competitiveness is precisely why parts of Europe have hesitated to ratify the MERCOSUR treaty. That treaty has now opened the European market to Brazil and its MERCOSUR partners.

A stable place in an unstable world

Wars in Europe, the Middle East, and Africa have exposed the geopolitical vulnerability of Europe: supply chains interrupted or under threat — think of wheat from Ukraine, or petroleum, natural gas, and fertilizers in the spring of 2026.

Brazil, by contrast, is a country at peace with itself and its neighbours. No power has a strategic interest against it, and it is too big to be pushed around. It is self-sufficient in both energy and food.

Safety of investment

Private property is protected by the Brazilian constitution. Foreign ownership of land is allowed.

Because of the sheer size of the country — and the special demands of regions like the Amazon — there is a defined bureaucracy for registering a land purchase correctly:

  • a land registry entry,
  • enrolment in the rural environmental register (CAR, cadastro ambiental rural), declaring cultivated and protected areas,
  • a geodesic verification measurement of the property,
  • and further registrations, for example for tax purposes.

A lawyer should always be involved in any land purchase. The reward for this diligence: the purchaser receives an indisputable title to the land, with all the rights the constitution guarantees.

Protection of the environment

Brazil protects its environment through mandatory conservation zones on agricultural properties:

  • Reserva Legal — a fixed percentage of every property must remain untouched. In the Amazon zone this is 80 percent; in the wet savannah of Tocantins it is 35 percent. The remainder may be put to productive use.
  • APP (áreas de proteção permanente, permanent protection areas) — buffer zones around watercourses, springs, and hilltops; for example 50 m around springs and 30 m along smaller rivers. In practice, APP covers some 3–5% of a property.
Important to know: these conservation rules are not dead weight — they are the point. A farm here keeps a real share of natural vegetation standing: habitat, watercourses, and carbon, preserved alongside productive land. Extensive cattle grazing is still permitted within the Reserva Legal and the APP, and — as the Carbon Credits page explains — protecting this land feeds directly into a state-wide environmental programme. Farming in Tocantins means working the land and safeguarding nature at the same time.
